What It Is

The visualizer is the row of bars that moves with the music on the Media Room page. Low notes are on the left, high notes on the right, and a bar is tall when that pitch is loud at this moment of the song.

The audio analyzer is the part of OCTAVE that works out those bars. It listens to what is playing, a song from your library or the sound of a mirrored phone, and gives out 96 numbers, one per bar, a few times a second. It draws nothing itself: the Media Room draws the bars, and any other screen or program can ask for the same numbers.

This page starts with how to use it and gets more technical as it goes.

Where You See It

Open the Media Room (the Now Playing page) and play something. The bars sit just above the seek bar, as wide as it is, in the theme's accent colour. There is one look: 96 rounded bars that fade when quiet and brighten when loud.

What you seeWhy
Bars movingMusic is playing and the analyzer has its audio.
Bars flat for a moment after a track changeThe new song is being read. They rise when that is done.
Bars flat while pausedThey only move while something plays.
Bars flat with Spotify or Android AutoThat audio does not pass through the analyzer. See Limits.
No bars at allThe visualizer is switched off, or there is nothing to play (an empty library and no other source).

The Music Library page and the Home screen have no visualizer.

The number of bars, their shape and their speed are fixed. The colour follows the theme. Every other setting is in the Settings Reference.

What It Costs

The visualizer costs two things, and both stop when it is off or off screen:

  • Reading each song once. When a track starts, OCTAVE decodes the whole file on a background thread to work out its bars. The screen does not wait for it, but on a small computer it is a burst of work at every track change. It starts only after the Media Room's track-change animation has finished, so the two do not compete.
  • Drawing while music plays. The bars change up to 10 times a second (20 for phone audio) and each change is eased over 120 ms, so the screen redraws continuously while a song plays. On a small computer this is the larger cost.

Memory is small: the bars of the current song only, about 1 KB per second of music (roughly 170 KB for a three-minute song). Nothing is cached on disk.

To turn it down: there is no in-between setting. If the Media Room stutters on your device, switch Waveform off. No song is decoded and nothing is drawn after that. The same is true whenever the Media Room is not on screen: the analyzer does nothing unless somebody is showing its bars.

How It Works

From here on the page is for people who want to know what happens underneath, or who are changing the code.

Sound, to a computer, is a long list of numbers called samples: the position of the loudspeaker cone, measured tens of thousands of times a second. Samples say how loud the sound is but not which notes are in it. To get bars the analyzer has to turn a short stretch of samples into "how loud is each pitch". The tool for that is the FFT (fast Fourier transform): give it a slice of samples and it returns the strength of every frequency in that slice, from the lowest to the highest.

The analyzer works in two ways, depending on what plays:

SourceHow
A song from the libraryAhead of time. The whole file is analysed once when the track starts. During playback the analyzer only looks up the row of bars for the current position.
A mirrored phoneLive. There is no file, so the analyzer keeps the last second of sound that went to the speakers and analyses the newest part of it 20 times a second.

Both give the same result: 96 whole numbers from 0 to 8, one per bar, 0 for silent and 8 for full height. In QML and over the service API this is audioAnalyzer.levels.

From a Song to Bars

For a song from the library, on a background thread:

  1. Decode. Qt's audio decoder turns the file (MP3, FLAC, M4A and so on) into samples, a buffer at a time. Which formats work depends on the platform's decoder, the same one that plays the song.
  2. Make it small. Left and right are averaged into one channel, and only every Nth sample is kept, bringing the rate down to about 8,000 samples a second (every 5th sample of a 44.1 kHz file, every 6th of a 48 kHz one). A visualizer does not need more, and it makes everything after this several times cheaper. It also means the bars show pitches up to about 4 kHz: the body of the music, not the highest treble.
  3. Cut it into slices of a tenth of a second. Each slice becomes one row of bars, so a song has 10 rows per second.
  4. Run the FFT on each slice. The slice is first faded in and out at its ends (a Hann window), so that the cut itself does not show up as sound. The FFT then gives the strength of 512 frequencies, each about 8 Hz apart.
  5. Group the frequencies into 96 bars. Each bar is the average of its frequencies. The first 64 bars take one frequency each (up to 500 Hz, where bass and most of the melody live); the last 32 get wider and wider and cover 500 Hz to 4 kHz. Giving the low end more bars matches how we hear pitch.
  6. Scale to 0–8. When the whole song is done, the analyzer finds a loudness that 95 % of all values are below and calls it "full height". Using the very loudest value would make one cymbal crash flatten the rest of the song. Values are then lifted by a curve (power 0.6) so quiet passages still show, and rounded down to a whole number from 0 to 8.

The result is one byte per bar per slice, kept in memory until the next track replaces it. Because the scale comes from the song itself, a quiet recording and a loud one both fill the bars.

Skipping quickly. One song is analysed at a time. If you skip while one is being read, the analyzer remembers only the newest request, throws the finished result away and reads the song that is actually playing. The bars stay flat meanwhile; they never show the previous song's data.

Where the Samples Come From

The analyzer follows what is playing by itself. nowPlaying tells it which source has the music and whether it plays; mediaManager tells it the current file and position. This wiring is in src/appcore.cpp. A screen never has to tell the analyzer what to analyse.

SourceSamples fromNotes
Local fileThe file itself, decoded once as above.All platforms.
Local file in the browser demoThe browser decodes the song (Web Audio) and hands over mono samples at 8 kHz. The rest is the same.Qt has no decoder in the browser. Only songs from a folder you picked on the page.
Mirrored phoneThe phone's sound as OCTAVE plays it: every piece written to the speakers is also given to the analyzer.Computers only, where phone mirroring runs.
Spotify, Android AutoNothing.The bars stay flat.

Phone audio, live

Phone sound arrives as a stream, so there is nothing to analyse ahead of time. The analyzer reduces it the same way (one channel, 8,000 samples a second) and keeps the last second. Every 50 ms it takes the tenth of a second that is being heard right now and runs the same FFT and the same 96 bars on it. "Being heard" matters: sound waits a moment in the speaker's buffer after OCTAVE writes it, so the analyzer steps back by that much to keep the bars in time with your ears.

A stream has no "whole song" to take the scale from. So "full height" follows the music: it jumps up at once when the sound gets louder and sinks back over about a second. Below a floor (very quiet sound) the bars stay flat, so silence is not blown up into noise. If no sound has arrived for a quarter of a second, the bars drop to zero.

How the Bars Reach the Screen

  1. Somebody asks for bars. A screen says its bars are visible with audioAnalyzer.setDemand(name, true). The Media Room uses the shortcut setViewActive(true). Until at least one asks, the analyzer decodes nothing and sends nothing.
  2. The player reports its position. While a local song plays, mediaManager reports where it is every 100 ms. The analyzer picks the row for that position: row number = seconds × 10. A seek simply picks another row.
  3. The analyzer sends the row, if it changed. The signal is fftDataChanged(levels), which is also what updates the levels property. A row equal to the last one sends nothing. That gives at most 10 updates a second for a local song and 20 for phone audio.
  4. The screen eases between rows. Ten rows a second would look like steps, so the Media Room animates each bar's height over 120 ms. The smoothness comes from the screen, not from the analyzer.

When the music pauses, or the last screen stops asking, the analyzer sends one row of zeros so the bars fall flat. A position past the analysed part of a song also gives zeros.

Track changes in the Media Room. The page drops its bars to zero at once and tells the analyzer its bars are "not visible" while the album art animates. The new song is decoded when the animation ends, and the bars rise when its analysis lands.

Drawing Your Own Visualizer

You need three things: say when your bars are visible, under a name of your own; read levels; draw. This is a complete visualizer in QML: 

Row {
    id: bars
    height: 60
    spacing: 1

    // Ask for bars only while we are on screen
    onVisibleChanged: audioAnalyzer.setDemand("myBars", visible)
    Component.onCompleted: audioAnalyzer.setDemand("myBars", visible)
    Component.onDestruction: audioAnalyzer.setDemand("myBars", false)

    Repeater {
        id: repeater
        model: audioAnalyzer.numBars            // 96
        Rectangle {
            property int level: 0               // 0 to 8
            width: 4
            height: Math.max(2, level / 8 * bars.height)
            anchors.bottom: parent.bottom
            color: App.Style.accent
            Behavior on height { NumberAnimation { duration: 120 } }
        }
    }

    Connections {
        target: audioAnalyzer
        function onFftDataChanged(levels) {
            for (var i = 0; i < repeater.count; i++)
                repeater.itemAt(i).level = levels[i] || 0
        }
    }
}
  • Use setDemand with your own name, not setViewActive: that one is the Media Room's, and turning it off would stop its bars.
  • Always take the demand back when you are hidden or destroyed. A forgotten demand keeps songs being decoded for nobody.
  • Write the levels in one handler as above. A binding to audioAnalyzer.levels[index] in each bar also works, but converts the whole list once per bar on every update.
  • You need not draw 96 bars or bars at all. Average neighbours for fewer bars, or drive a circle, a line or a glow from the same numbers.

From another program, over the service API: call audioAnalyzer.setDemand("myApp", true), then subscribe to the levels property. Add throttleMs to the subscription if you want fewer updates. If your connection closes, the demand is taken back for you. setDemand and reading levels need only the read permission. See Writing a Frontend.

Limits

  • Spotify and Android Auto have no bars. Their sound is not given to the analyzer.
  • Phone bars on computers only. The phone's sound reaches the analyzer only where phone mirroring plays it.
  • Up to about 4 kHz. Higher treble is not shown as its own bars. The reduction to 8,000 samples a second is a plain "keep every Nth sample", so the bars are a picture of the music, not a measurement.
  • The first 3 hours of a file. A longer file is analysed up to there; after that the bars are flat.
  • A file that cannot be decoded has flat bars. The song still plays. The analyzer gives a decode 30 seconds (60 in the browser) before it gives up.
  • One song at a time, nothing kept. Going back to a song you heard a minute ago reads it again.
  • In the browser demo only songs from a folder picked on the page are analysed.

When the Bars Stay Flat

  1. Check the source. Spotify and Android Auto never move the bars. Paused music does not either.
  2. Check the switch: Waveform in the Media Room's look editor.
  3. Read the log. A failed analysis writes a warning to octave-cpp.log under the category octave.audioanalyzer: Analysis failed for <file>, preceded by the reason (QAudioDecoder error, timed out after 30 s or produced no usable audio data). See Architecture for where the logs are.
  4. Try another file format. If only some files fail, the platform's decoder does not handle them.

Why It Is Built This Way

  • Local songs are analysed ahead of time. The cost is paid once per track, off the main thread. During playback a row lookup costs nothing, on any device.
  • The worker shares nothing. The background analysis gets a file path and returns finished bars. Only the main thread touches the analyzer's state, so there are no locks. The live phone path runs entirely on the main thread; it is one small FFT every 50 ms.
  • Decode a buffer, reduce it, forget it. Holding a whole decoded track in memory can freeze a small computer on an hour-long mix. Only the bar values are kept while decoding.
  • The decoder is asked for nothing special. The analyzer takes whatever rate and format the platform's decoder produces and does the mixing and reduction itself. Asking the decoder to convert has been seen to produce no audio at all for MP3, with no error.
  • The analyzer does not animate. It sends a row only when it changes, and whole numbers from 0 to 8 make "did it change" cheap. Easing between rows is the screen's job.
  • The analyzer drives itself. What is playing, where it is and whether to run are rules every screen would otherwise repeat. A screen only says whether its bars are visible.
  • Its own FFT. A 1024-point transform ten times per second of music does not justify another library on every platform. The analyzer needs only Qt Multimedia, which the player already uses.
  • One switch, no quality levels. The bar count is a constant (96), so every screen and every client can rely on it.
