What It Does

Every car sold since the late 1990s has an OBD-II port, usually under the dashboard. Plug a small ELM327 adapter into it and OCTAVE can ask the car's computer questions: engine speed, coolant temperature, fuel level, what the check-engine light is about. The OBD manager is the part of OCTAVE that holds that conversation. Everything on screen that shows a number from the car gets it from here.

It works with any ELM327-compatible adapter: Bluetooth or USB on Linux, Windows, macOS and the Raspberry Pi, Bluetooth LE on Android, and Bluetooth LE or USB in the browser demo. There is also a simulated car, so you can try all of it with no adapter at all.

This page starts with how to use it and gets more technical as it goes. Stop reading whenever you have what you came for.

Connecting a Car

  1. Plug the adapter into the car's OBD-II port and turn the ignition on. Most adapters only have power with the ignition on.
  2. In OCTAVE, open the OBD section. With no car connected it shows "No vehicle connected" and a Connect Vehicle button.
  3. Tap it. The Connect Vehicle sheet opens and lists what it can find.
  4. Tap your adapter. OCTAVE remembers it and reconnects by itself from then on.

What the sheet can do depends on the device OCTAVE runs on:

DeviceHow to connect
Linux, Raspberry Pi, Orange PiTap Pick Bluetooth Adapter. OCTAVE scans, pairs (it tries the usual PINs for you) and connects. You do not need to pair in the system settings first. A USB adapter appears in the list by itself.
Windows, macOSPair the adapter in the system's Bluetooth settings first, then pick it from the sheet. A USB adapter appears in the list by itself.
AndroidPair the adapter in Android's Bluetooth settings first. The first time you connect, Android asks for the Bluetooth permission: allow it, then pick the adapter again. The adapter must be a Bluetooth LE one.
Browser demoPick Bluetooth Adapter (a Bluetooth LE adapter) or Pick USB Adapter, in Chrome or Edge. See Browser Demo.
Any deviceSimulated car is always in the sheet. See Simulated Car.

If the sheet finds nothing, plug the adapter in, turn the ignition on and scan again. If your adapter has an unusual name, tap Show All Devices: the sheet normally lists only devices that look like OBD adapters. You can also type an address by hand in Settings > OBD > Connection > Enter Address Manually: a serial port (/dev/ttyUSB0, COM3) or a Bluetooth address (AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF).

How It Works

From here on the page is for people who want to know what happens underneath, or who are changing the code.

An ELM327 is a simple device. You send it a line of text, it asks the car, and it sends a line of text back. It can only do one thing at a time. To ask for engine speed OCTAVE sends 010C ("mode 01, reading 0C") and gets back something like 41 0C 1A F8, which decodes to 1726 RPM. Each kind of reading has a number like 0C, called a PID.

Four pieces do the work:

PieceJob
OBDManagerThe part the rest of the app talks to (obdManager in QML and the service API). It decides when to connect, what to ask for and what the status is. It runs on the main thread and never waits on the adapter.
OBDConnectionWorkerDoes the actual talking, on its own thread, so a slow adapter never freezes the screen. It owns the port from the moment it opens until it closes.
ELM327ProtocolKnows the language: how to write each request and decode each reply. No I/O, so it is tested without a car.
paramStoreHolds the latest value of every reading. Gauges read from here, never from the manager.
 
  gauges, cards, Home          black box
        ^                          ^
        |  latest value            |  every reading
  +-----+------+                   |
  | paramStore | <-----------------+------ parameterSampled(id, value)
  +------------+                                    ^
                                                    |
                                          +---------+---------+
                                          |    OBDManager     |  main thread
                                          +---------+---------+
                                                    |  requests and results, queued
                                          +---------+---------+
                                          | OBDConnectionWorker|  its own thread
                                          +---------+---------+
                                                    |  text over serial / Bluetooth
                                          ELM327 adapter -> OBD-II port -> car

Android is the one exception: there the worker is replaced by a small Java Bluetooth LE helper, described under Android. The rest of the app cannot tell the difference.

How a Connection Is Made

  1. Look for the adapter. At startup, and every 10 seconds while not connected, the manager lists the ports that could be an adapter (the list per platform is below). If the saved adapter is not among them the status is Device Not Found.
  2. Open it. A new worker opens the port at 38400 baud, or opens a Bluetooth socket (Direct Bluetooth).
  3. Set the adapter up. Seven commands: reset (ATZ), echo off, linefeeds off, spaces off, headers off, adaptive timing, automatic protocol (ATSP0). A command with no answer is sent once more.
  4. Check that a car is there. The worker asks for engine speed and waits up to 20 seconds, because this first request is when the adapter searches for the car's protocol. Only a real RPM reply counts. A car with no engine speed (some electric cars) is asked which readings it supports instead. No answer means No Vehicle, and the worker lets go of the port.
  5. Read the VIN once, and work out the make and model year from it (vin, vehicleMake, vehicleModelYear). Many older cars do not report one; the fields stay empty.
  6. Start polling. The status becomes Connected.
  7. Ask the car what it supports, two seconds later. The answer (vehicleSupportedParameters) decides which cards Live Data shows and removes unsupported readings from the poll list. It describes the car connected right now: it is cleared on every disconnect and never loaded from a previous session.

Where the manager looks for adapters

PlatformLooked at
Linux / Pi/dev/rfcomm*, /dev/ttyUSB*, /dev/ttyACM*. Not /dev/ttyS*: hardware serial ports are almost never OBD adapters.

macOS/dev/tty.* and /dev/cu.* entries named OBD, Bluetooth, usbserial or ELM.

The result is obdManager.availableAdapters: a list of {name, identifier, kind}, with a readable name such as "USB ELM327 · /dev/ttyUSB0". The same list is used on every platform, so the screens never need to know which platform they are on. To connect to one, call obdManager.connectToAdapter(identifier): it saves the adapter and connects at once.

Finding and Pairing an Adapter

On desktop Linux (the Pi and Orange Pi included) OCTAVE can find and pair a Bluetooth adapter itself, so nobody has to open the system's Bluetooth settings or know a MAC address. This is obdAdapterFinder, and it is what the Connect Vehicle sheet drives:

  1. Scan for 15 seconds. Devices whose name looks like an ELM327 (OBDII, V-LINK, OBDLink, Vgate, Veepeak, NEXAS and so on) are marked as likely adapters and shown first.
  2. Pair. OCTAVE answers the PIN request itself, trying 1234, 0000, 6789 and 1111 in turn, so no system dialog appears.
  3. Trust the adapter, so it reconnects later without asking. If pairing fails but the adapter answered, OCTAVE tries to connect anyway: some adapters accept a trusted host without pairing.
  4. Find the channel the adapter's serial service is on (channel 1 if it does not say).
  5. Connect with obdManager.connectDirect(mac, channel) and save the adapter under its name.

On other platforms the finder reports supported: false and the sheet lists availableAdapters instead. Bluetooth LE adapters are listed but greyed out on desktop, which cannot talk to them yet; they work on Android and in the browser. OCTAVE never waits for the Bluetooth service at startup: if it is not running, the sheet says so and the rest of the app carries on.

Direct Bluetooth (Linux)

There are two ways to reach a Bluetooth Classic adapter on Linux. The old way binds it to a device file (/dev/rfcomm0) with the rfcomm tool, which usually needs root. The direct way opens a Bluetooth socket straight to the adapter. Direct is what the finder uses, and it is better in three ways: it needs no root, it fails with a real error ("Host is down") within a few seconds, and no other program can take the link away. A direct adapter is saved as rfcomm://<MAC>, or rfcomm://<MAC>/<channel> when the channel is not 1. The Direct button in Settings > OBD does the same with an address you type.

If you give OCTAVE a bare MAC address the old way is used: it runs rfcomm bind for you, or uses an existing /dev/rfcomm0 to 7 if there is one. If that fails the status is Error and the Connection Log says how to fix it (usually: add your user to the bluetooth group).

Which Readings Get Polled

The adapter answers one question at a time, and a round trip takes tens of milliseconds. Asking for all 120 readings in turn would make every gauge crawl. So OCTAVE only asks for what is on screen.

Each page tells the manager which readings it shows: obdManager.setParameterDemand("myPage", ids). An empty list takes the request back. The manager joins everyone's lists into one poll list and the worker goes round it. Switching pages changes the list a quarter of a second later, with no reconnect. The rules:

  • Readings the car does not support are left out, once the car has said what it supports.
  • Engine speed is always polled, whatever is on screen. It keeps the shift light working everywhere and proves the link is alive. On a car with no engine speed, the adapter's voltage reading takes its place.
  • Readings that do not come from the car (pitch, roll, heading and the other IMU values) are ignored here.

There is a second, low-priority list: setBackgroundDemand(consumer, ids). The worker polls one background reading after each full round of the on-screen list. The black box uses it to record everything the car supports, at the cost of one extra request per round instead of slowing every gauge.

Fast polling

With obdFastMode on (the default) the worker asks again as soon as an answer arrives, and uses two tricks:

  • It tells the adapter to expect one reply (010C1), so the adapter answers at once and does not wait to see if another computer in the car replies too. On Bluetooth LE this wait is most of the round trip.
  • On cars with a CAN bus (most cars since 2008) it asks for up to six readings in one request.

Both are dropped for the rest of the connection if the adapter or the car does not understand them. With fast polling off the worker waits 50 ms between requests and asks for one reading at a time. Every 30 seconds the worker writes the poll rate to the log (OBD polling: N requests in 30 s ...).

Staying Connected

A car is a rough place for a connection: the ignition goes off, the adapter loses power, Bluetooth drops. Three things keep OCTAVE connected without anyone touching it.

  • The 10-second scan. While not connected, the manager looks for the saved adapter every 10 seconds and connects when it appears. This is what reconnects you when you start the car, and it works with Auto Reconnect at 0. An adapter that answers while the car does not (ignition off) is tried again on the same tick.
  • Auto Reconnect (obdAutoReconnectAttempts, 1 to 10). After a failure the manager retries after 4, 6, 8, then 10 seconds, up to the number you set, then goes back to the 10-second scan. It is off by default because the scan is enough for most setups.
  • The watchdog. Every 5 seconds while connected, the manager checks when the last reading arrived. After 30 seconds with none, the status is Stale Connection and the connection is made again from scratch.

When a live link drops by itself, connectionLostUnexpectedly(reason) is emitted and the screen shows a notice. Disconnecting on purpose, changing adapter, and the watchdog's quiet restart do not show one.

Rule: never hold a port open that nobody is answering on
On Linux, a Bluetooth serial port whose other end is gone looks permanently "readable" while delivering no bytes, and Qt's serial port will spin a whole CPU core on it. So the worker closes the port on every failure: it would not open, the adapter did not answer, or the car did not answer. It also treats 50 empty reads in a row as a dead adapter. If the obd-worker thread ever shows up hot in top -H, check for a held port first: ls -l /proc/<pid>/fd | grep rfcomm.

What Can Be Read

OCTAVE knows 129 readings. Every one has an id in capitals, such as RPM or COOLANT_TEMP, and that id is what you use everywhere: in a dashboard, in setParameterDemand, in the black box's files.

GroupCountExamples
Standard readings83RPM, SPEED, COOLANT_TEMP, ENGINE_LOAD, THROTTLE_POS, FUEL_LEVEL, INTAKE_TEMP, MAF, fuel trims, oxygen sensors, catalyst temperatures, and ELM_VOLTAGE (battery voltage measured by the adapter itself)
Newer readings37Found on newer cars: torque, BOOST_PRESSURE_A, ODOMETER, TRANSMISSION_GEAR_RATIO, fuel rate, hybrid battery voltage and current
Alias1IGNITION_TIMING, another name for TIMING_ADVANCE
IMU8PITCH, ROLL, HEADING and so on, from the BerryIMU, not from the car

The full list, with each reading's title, unit and range, is in the API reference and in docs/GAUGE_AUTHORING.md. Worth knowing:

  • No car supports all of them. An older car may answer 15; a new one 60.
  • Raw values are in °C and mph. Gauges show them in the units chosen in Settings > Display (°F, km/h).
  • Boost pressure is absolute: subtract BAROMETRIC_PRESSURE for what a boost gauge shows.
  • Some newer PIDs carry several readings in one reply (PID 70 gives both actual and commanded boost). They are polled once and decoded into each reading.

Values leave the manager in one signal, parameterSampled(id, value), fired once for every decoded reading. paramStore and the black box both listen to it. The store passes values on to the screen at most about 30 times a second, which is as fast as a needle needs. To read a value in QML use OBDParameterModel.liveValue(id); see Gauges & Dashboards.

To add a standard reading to OCTAVE: add its decoder to the table in elm327protocol.cpp and a row to parametercatalog.cpp. A test fails if you add one without the other.

Trouble Codes

OBD > Trouble Codes reads what is behind the check-engine light: the stored codes, the pending ones (seen once, not yet confirmed), whether the light is on, and the freeze frame, which is the set of readings the car saved at the moment the fault happened. Each code comes with a description: its meaning for common codes, otherwise what the code's letters and digits say (which system, generic or make-specific). Clear erases the codes and turns the light off. If the fault is still there, the light comes back.

The adapter can do only one thing at a time, so while the Trouble Codes page is open, live readings stop. The page calls enterDiagnosticMode() when it appears and exitDiagnosticMode() when it goes. On leaving, OCTAVE reconnects from scratch (status Reconnecting, about a second) so the adapter is back in a known state; it tries up to three times even with Auto Reconnect at 0.

Trouble codes are not available on Android yet.

Black Box: Trip Recorder

OCTAVE records every reading of every drive, with its time. You do not start or stop it. Trip History (OBD > Trip History) shows the drives; see Pages for the viewer.

  • A drive starts at the first reading with the engine running.
  • A drive ends after 2 minutes with the engine stopped, 2 minutes without the adapter, when you turn recording off, or when OCTAVE closes. A shorter dropout is noted in the drive and does not split it.
  • Everything is recorded, not only what is on screen: the recorder asks for every reading the car supports through the background list. Turn "Record everything the car reports" off to record only what the screen asked for.
  • A power cut loses at most 2 seconds. Readings are written to disk every 2 seconds. A drive cut short is finished off at the next start.
  • Events are kept too: disconnects, the check-engine light coming on, codes read and cleared.

A value that is not changing is stored once every 5 seconds, so a steady reading does not fill the disk. Expect about 3 MB per hour of driving.

The files

Under history/ in the config folder:

FileWhat

Export in Trip History copies a drive's two files to Downloads/OCTAVE-trips/, which is how you get one off a phone. On a computer, Open folder opens trips/. When the storage cap is reached the oldest drives are deleted. If the database cannot be opened (a full or read-only disk), the Black Box card says "unavailable" with the reason, and nothing is recorded.

Shift Light

A shift light tells you when to change gear without looking at the tachometer. In OCTAVE it is a list of flags. Each flag is a range of engine speed, a colour and an effect. Tap the RPM card in Live Data to edit them.

EffectWhat you see
LampA coloured light on the RPM card and on the Shift Light dashboard widget.
FlashThe light blinks, at a speed you set.
Full-screen flashThe whole screen is tinted in the flag's colour. Touches still go through.
AuraA glow around the edges of the screen that leaves the middle readable.

When ranges overlap, the flag lower in the list wins. The full-screen flash and the aura appear on every page, not just the OBD one, which is why engine speed is always polled. The rules live in C++ (shiftLightManager): the screens only draw its activeFlag and lit, so every lamp and overlay blinks in step. The flags are stored as JSON in the rpm_flags setting; each is {rpmLow, rpmHigh, color, flash, flashSpeed, fullScreenFlash, fullScreenFlashOpacity, auraFlash, auraFlashOpacity, auraSpread}.

Custom PIDs

The standard readings are the ones every make agreed on. Your car knows much more (transmission temperature, oil level, individual wheel speeds), but each make keeps those under its own numbers. If you know the number and the formula for your car (forums and Torque PID lists are the usual source), you can add it.

Custom readings live in custom-pids.json in the config folder. Settings > OBD > Advanced > Custom PIDs has a Create starter file button and shows any entry that was skipped and why. Each entry is an id, a title and unit, the request, optionally which computer in the car to ask (header), and a formula over the reply's bytes such as A-40 or (A*256+B)/10. The file format and the formula language are in docs/CUSTOM_PIDS.md.

  • A custom reading behaves like any other: it has a Live Data card, works in a dashboard and is recorded by the black box.
  • The file is read once at startup. Restart OCTAVE after editing it.
  • Read only. Only read requests (modes 22 and 21) are accepted, and OCTAVE builds the request itself from the numbers in the file. Nothing in the file is sent to the car as written.
  • After asking another computer in the car, the worker always points the adapter back at the default one before doing anything else.
  • A reading that goes unanswered three times in a row is then tried only every 30 seconds.
  • Not on Android yet.

Bus Monitor

The computers in a car talk to each other all the time on a shared network, the bus. The Bus Monitor (Settings > OBD > Advanced) shows that traffic: one row per message id, how often it comes, its latest bytes, and which bytes just changed. It is for finding out which message carries what: press the brake and see which byte moves. The guide is docs/BUS_MONITOR.md.

  • Listen only. The adapter is put in its monitor mode, in which it transmits nothing. OCTAVE has no way to send a message to the bus.
  • While it listens, the adapter cannot answer questions, so live readings and the black box pause. Any other request (trouble codes, a custom PID) ends listening.
  • A filter narrows it to one message id or a pattern (hex digits, with X for "any").
  • Recording writes a log under history/bus/ in the format candump, SavvyCAN and Wireshark read.
  • A busy bus is faster than the adapter's link (about 200 messages a second at 38400 baud). The adapter then reports BUFFER FULL; OCTAVE counts it and carries on. Use a filter.
  • Many cars built since about 2010 keep their internal traffic behind a gateway, so the OBD port stays silent. That is the car, not OCTAVE.
  • Not on Android. No recording in the browser demo.

Android

On Android, OCTAVE talks to a Bluetooth LE adapter through a small Java helper (OctaveOBDBridge.java) and not through the worker. Qt's own Bluetooth support refused to work on recent Android versions even with every permission granted, so OCTAVE goes straight to Android's Bluetooth API.

  • Adapters: Bluetooth LE ELM327s. The helper recognises the three common kinds without any setting (ISSC, FFF0 and FFE0 services: NEXAS, Vgate iCar Pro, OBDLink CX, Konnwei, Veepeak and HM-10 based clones).
  • Pairing: pair in Android's settings. OCTAVE lists paired devices and does not scan.
  • Permission: the first connection asks for Bluetooth permission. Allow it and pick the adapter again.
  • Same readings, same polling rules as everywhere else, including the black box.
  • Reconnecting: after a dropped link it retries up to 3 times, then waits for you.

Not on Android yet: trouble codes, custom PIDs, the bus monitor.

Extra statuses appear while connecting on Android: Connecting BLE..., Discovering services..., Initializing ELM327..., Querying PIDs..., and for failures No MAC Address, BLE connect failed and BLE error.

Browser Demo

The browser build can talk to a real adapter in Chrome or Edge:

  • Pick Bluetooth Adapter: a Bluetooth LE ELM327, over Web Bluetooth. On Linux this needs Chrome's experimental web platform features flag; the sheet says so.
  • Pick USB Adapter: an adapter on a USB cable, over Web Serial, on a computer.

Both feed the same worker as the desktop app, so everything on this page applies. The page opens with no car; pick "Simulated car" to see it move.

Simulated Car

Simulated car in the Connect Vehicle sheet is a made-up car that drives itself. It is always there, on every platform, with Bluetooth off. It is not a set of fake numbers painted on the screen: it is a simulated ELM327 plugged in underneath the real manager, so the real connection, polling, decoding, trouble codes, custom PIDs and bus monitor all run. That makes it the right way to try OCTAVE, to build a dashboard, or to develop a frontend without sitting in a car.

  • Settings > OBD shows "Simulated" beside the status, and obdManager.simulated is true.
  • Drives recorded from it are marked "simulated car" in Trip History.
  • Your real adapter stays saved. Picking it again, or restarting, goes back to it.
  • From code: obdManager.connectToAdapter("sim://elm327").
  • From the command line: octave --sim, or octave --headless --sim with no screen. See Development.

When Something Goes Wrong

  1. The Connection Log (Settings > OBD > Connection). Look for the "Vehicle check" line: "no answer" means the adapter is not talking to the car, which is nearly always the ignition.
  2. The log files. Failed connects, lost links, the poll rate and Android's Bluetooth events are written to octave-cpp.log without any special setting. See Architecture for where the logs are.
  3. The full conversation. Run with --debug and every line sent to and received from the adapter is logged (TX / RX, with the round-trip time) under the octave.elm327 category.

Common problems and their fixes are on the Troubleshooting page.

Why It Is Built This Way

  • A worker thread that blocks. The worker sends a command and waits for the answer. That is far simpler than a state machine, and the adapter can only do one thing at a time anyway. Being on its own thread, the waiting never touches the screen.
  • Poll what is on screen. The adapter's speed is the limit. Polling by demand is what makes a gauge smooth; the background list is what lets the black box still record everything.
  • One table of PIDs. The requests, the decoders, the vehicle scan and the simulated car all read the same table, so they cannot disagree.
  • Trouble codes pause live data and reconnect afterwards. Mixing the two on one adapter corrupts both. A fresh connection afterwards costs a second and always leaves the adapter in a known state.
  • Auto Reconnect is off by default. The 10-second scan already reconnects when the adapter appears, which is the usual case: the head unit boots before the car is started.
  • One adapter list for every platform. The differences between a COM port, a device file and a Bluetooth address stay inside the manager, so the screens are the same everywhere.
  • Failures must reach the log files. The head unit is in a car, with no terminal. The Connection Log and the log files exist so a failure on the road can be understood afterwards.
  • The simulated car sits below the real manager. Simulating the adapter, not the values, means the simulator exercises the code that runs in the car.
