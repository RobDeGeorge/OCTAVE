What It Does

OCTAVE can put your phone on the car's screen. You see the phone's picture, you hear its sound through the car's speakers at OCTAVE's volume, and a touch on OCTAVE's screen is a touch on the phone. There are three ways to do it, and which one you use depends on the phone:

WayPhoneWhat you getWhat it needs
Phone MirrorAndroidThe whole phone, or a separate desktop the phone draws for OCTAVE. Any app. Real multitouch. OCTAVE's music controls drive the phone's music.USB debugging switched on, on the phone. A cable the first time; Wi-Fi after that.
Android AutoAndroidThe car screen Android Auto draws: Maps, music apps, messages.Android Auto's developer mode on the phone, and a head-unit certificate you supply. Beta.
iPhone (AirPlay)iPhoneThe iPhone's screen and sound, as on an Apple TV. Touch through a Bluetooth pointer.The same Wi-Fi network. Nothing to install on the iPhone.

All three run on desktop builds only: Linux (the Raspberry Pi and Orange Pi included), Windows and macOS for Phone Mirror and Android Auto, Linux only for AirPlay. None of them is in the Android, iOS or browser builds. Nothing here uses the internet: the picture, the sound and the touches stay between the phone and OCTAVE.

This page starts with how to use each one and gets more technical as it goes. Stop reading whenever you have what you came for.

Phone Mirror (Android)

Phone Mirror shows an Android phone inside OCTAVE. It works through adb, the tool Android developers use to talk to a phone, so the phone needs USB debugging switched on. Nothing is installed on the phone: OCTAVE sends a small helper program to it each time and the phone runs it only while mirroring.

Connecting a Phone

  1. On the phone, once: open Settings > About phone and tap Build number seven times to unlock Developer options. In Settings > Developer options, turn on USB debugging.
  2. Plug the phone into the OCTAVE machine with a USB cable.
  3. On OCTAVE's Home screen, tap Connect Phone in the phone window. On Linux, plugging the phone in while Home is on screen starts mirroring by itself.
  4. On the phone, a prompt asks "Allow USB debugging?". Tick "Always allow from this computer" and tap Allow. The phone window says "Allow USB debugging on your phone" until you do.

From then on OCTAVE starts mirroring by itself whenever Home comes on screen and the phone is there. It also makes the phone reachable over Wi-Fi during that first cable session, so on Android 11 or newer you can pull the cable and carry on (see Without a Cable).

Without a Cable: Wi-Fi

The phone and OCTAVE must be on the same network. OCTAVE does not create one. At home that is your router. In a car it is usually an access point the head unit runs and the phone joins; the phone reports that network as having no internet and keeps using mobile data for its apps, which is what you want.

Tell OCTAVE about the car's network and it puts the phone on it. Type the network's name and password under Settings > Phone > Phone Mirror > Car Wi-Fi. From then on, when you plug a phone into the head unit, OCTAVE makes the phone join that network. You never pick it on the phone and nothing asks you to confirm. This happens in the driveway too, where both are on your Wi-Fi at home: the phone moves to the car's network, so mirroring does not stop when you drive out of reach of the house. While it is on the car's network the phone uses mobile data. Needs Android 11 or newer.

A computer that does not host the network itself (OCTAVE on a desk, say) only moves a phone it cannot reach over Wi-Fi. A phone on the same Wi-Fi as that computer is left there.

After that first cable session you do not need the cable. Android never rejoins a network with no internet by itself, so the head unit leaves a small helper on the phone, the watcher. When the phone has no Wi-Fi and the car's network comes in range, the watcher joins it. This works with the phone asleep in a pocket; allow up to two minutes then, and a few seconds when the phone is awake. OCTAVE finds the phone and starts mirroring.

The watcher does little: every ten seconds it asks the phone whether it is on Wi-Fi. It asks for a scan only while the phone is on no Wi-Fi at all, and it leaves a phone alone whose Wi-Fi is switched off or that is on another network. A phone restart ends it; plug the phone in once and it is back. To stop it, clear the Car Wi-Fi name: OCTAVE removes it from the phone the next time it connects.

There are three ways to make a phone reachable over Wi-Fi:

WayWhat you doGood to know
After a cable session (automatic)Nothing. Whenever a session starts on a USB phone with Android 11 or newer, OCTAVE also switches on the phone's Wireless debugging and connects to it.Pull the cable and mirroring carries on. With Car Wi-Fi set, the phone is put on the car's network first. A phone restart turns Wireless debugging off; one more cable session brings it back.
QR codeSettings > Phone > Phone Mirror > Wireless > Pair a Phone. On the phone: Settings > Developer options > Wireless debugging > Pair device with QR code, and point the camera at OCTAVE's screen.No cable at all. Android 11 or newer. The code expires after two minutes.
Stay Wireless After USB (a setting, off by default)Turn it on, then plug the phone in once.Works on older phones and when the phone itself is the hotspot. Until the phone restarts it accepts debugging connections on any network it joins, so use it only on networks you trust.

OCTAVE remembers the last phone it reached over Wi-Fi and reconnects to it by itself. It remembers the phone's address on each network it was reached on, the car's and the one at home, and tries every one that OCTAVE is on now. The Wireless card shows the most recent ("Remembered: …"). Forget disconnects it and stops reconnecting; to revoke it completely, also remove OCTAVE from the phone's paired devices under Wireless debugging. When a phone is on both a cable and Wi-Fi, the cable is used.

A phone that runs the hotspot itself cannot use Wireless debugging (Android only allows it while the phone is a Wi-Fi client), so the first two ways do not work there. Stay Wireless After USB does.

The Phone's Own Screen and Power Button

A sleeping phone sends no picture and ignores touches. So while it is mirrored, OCTAVE keeps the phone awake: it never dims or times out, and a press of the power button never stops mirroring. What the power button does to the phone's own screen depends on Phone Screen Off While Mirroring:

SettingThe phone's screenA press of the power button
Off (the default)Stays on.Turns the screen off, or on again, as on any phone. Mirroring continues either way.
OnDark while mirroring. Some phones only dim.Lights the screen for 8 seconds. Unlock it in that time, or press again, and the phone is yours: the screen stays on until you lock it or press power again. Otherwise it goes dark again.

The screen is put back the way it was when mirroring ends. If the phone locks itself a few seconds after its screen turns off (Samsung's default is 5 seconds), set that delay to "Immediately" for the most predictable behaviour.

Music From the Phone

While a phone is mirrored, whatever plays on it (Spotify, YouTube Music, a podcast app) is OCTAVE's music. The bottom bar, the Media Room, the music card on Home and the gesture sensor all show and control the phone's track: play, pause, next, previous and seek, with the title, artist and cover art. The music page lists the phone app's play queue, and a tap on an entry jumps to it.

  • The phone takes the controls as soon as mirroring starts, and OCTAVE's own music pauses. With no music app open the controls say "Phone" and "Nothing playing"; pressing play starts the phone's last music app.
  • Playing a local track hands the controls back to OCTAVE's player and pauses the phone. The phone takes them again the next time it starts playing.
  • Shuffle is dimmed for most phone apps: it belongs to the app.
  • Other sounds from the phone (a navigation prompt, a notification) turn OCTAVE's own music down while they play. See Duck Local Music.
  • The music survives a pulled cable. The phone keeps playing silently for up to 10 seconds while OCTAVE reconnects, so the song carries on after a short gap and never jumps to the phone's speaker.

Android Auto follows stricter rules about who may start music: see Which Source Plays the Music.

Android Auto

Android Auto is the car screen an Android phone draws when it is connected to a car: big buttons, Maps, music and messaging apps made for driving. In a car, the screen in the dashboard is the head unit. OCTAVE has two head units, chosen with Head Unit in Settings > Phone > Android Auto:

Head UnitWhat it isState
Built-inOCTAVE is the head unit itself and talks to the phone directly.Beta. Picture, touch, sound and music controls work. This is the one to use.
Google DHU (the default)OCTAVE starts Google's Desktop Head Unit, a developer tool from the Android SDK.The DHU opens in its own window, not inside OCTAVE. See Google DHU.

The Android Auto button appears in the bottom bar once Show in Nav Bar is on. The Head Unit choice is only shown in builds that have the built-in head unit; other builds always use Google DHU.

The Built-in Head Unit

With Head Unit set to Built-in, the phone runs Android Auto as it would in a car. It sends the car screen to OCTAVE as video and sound, and OCTAVE sends back your touches, whether its theme is light or dark, and that the car is parked. No Google software is needed on the OCTAVE machine.

The connection goes through Android Auto's head unit server, a developer feature of the Android Auto app, reached over adb. That is the same link Phone Mirror uses, so the phone needs USB debugging (or to be paired for Wi-Fi in Phone Mirror) and Android Auto's developer mode. Plugging in a stock phone with no developer settings, as you would in a car, is not supported yet, and neither is wireless Android Auto.

Setting It Up

On the phone, once:

  1. USB debugging on, exactly as for Phone Mirror.
  2. Developer mode in Android Auto: open Android Auto's settings on the phone and tap Version about ten times, until it confirms.
  3. Start the head unit server: in Android Auto's settings, open the ⋮ menu and tap Start head unit server. OCTAVE cannot start it for you.

In OCTAVE:

  1. Settings > Phone > Android Auto: turn on Show in Nav Bar.
  2. Set Head Unit to Built-in.
  3. Optionally pick a Video Size: 800x480, 1280x720 or 1920x1080. It applies at the next connection.
  4. Put your head-unit certificate where OCTAVE looks for it (next section).
  5. Tap the Android Auto button in the bottom bar. The page connects by itself as soon as the phone, its head unit server and the certificate are all there.

The first line of the Android Auto settings card shows the same status as the page, in green while connected and red when something is wrong.

The Head-Unit Certificate

A phone only accepts a head unit that proves itself with a certificate issued under Google's head-unit certificate authority. OCTAVE does not ship one: nothing in the repository or in the downloads contains one. You supply a certificate and its private key, both in PEM format, and you are responsible for having the right to use them. OCTAVE looks for them in this order:

  1. The file named by the androidAutoCertPath setting, if it is set. The key is taken from the same file when it contains one, otherwise from a file next to it with the same name and a .key ending (/path/unit.pem and /path/unit.key).
  2. Otherwise headunit.crt and headunit.key in the android-auto folder inside OCTAVE's config folder (on Linux ~/.config/OCTAVE/android-auto/). The page and the settings card show the full path.

The files are read at every connection attempt, so dropping them in while the page says "Android Auto needs a certificate" is enough: the next try, within 3 seconds, picks them up.

Which Source Plays the Music

OCTAVE's own player, Spotify, a mirrored phone and Android Auto can all play music, and exactly one of them owns the music at a time. Android Auto asks the head unit before it plays, so OCTAVE decides, and the rule is: only something you do changes the owner. Android Auto connecting, reconnecting, or its own "start music automatically" setting never does.

SituationWhat happens
Android Auto connects, or a music app on the phone starts by itselfRefused. The phone pauses and what was playing keeps playing.
You tap the Android Auto screen, press play / next / previous while it is the source, or ask by voice, and the phone asks to play within 6 secondsGranted. Android Auto owns the music and the previous source pauses.
Android Auto owns the music and asks again, after a pauseGranted.
You play something in OCTAVE's own playerOCTAVE's player owns the music. The phone pauses.
A navigation prompt or system sound from Android AutoOCTAVE's own music is turned down to 30 % while it plays.
Google Assistant opens the microphoneOCTAVE's own music pauses, and resumes when the microphone closes unless something else started.
OCTAVE restarts (car off and on) while Android Auto owned the music and was playingAndroid Auto resumes when it connects. If the phone has not started its music after 4 seconds, OCTAVE presses play. Playing anything local first cancels this.

A mirrored phone is simpler: it takes the controls as soon as mirroring starts (Music From the Phone). Bringing it under the same rules is planned in TODO/media-source-arbiter.md. From code, the owner is nowPlaying.source ("local", "phone", "androidauto" or "spotify"), and nowPlaying.useLocal() hands the music back to OCTAVE's player with nothing playing.

Google DHU

The DHU does not appear inside OCTAVE
With Head Unit set to Google DHU, OCTAVE finds and starts the Desktop Head Unit and sets up the adb link, but the DHU stays in its own window. OCTAVE's page does not show its picture, and touches on OCTAVE's screen do not reach it. Use the built-in head unit to see Android Auto inside OCTAVE.

To use it anyway:

  1. Install the DHU from Android Studio: Tools > SDK Manager > SDK Tools > Android Auto Desktop Head Unit Emulator. OCTAVE looks for it in the Android SDK's extras/google/auto/ folder.
  2. Connect the phone with USB debugging on, with Android Auto's developer mode on.
  3. Open the Android Auto page. OCTAVE forwards port 5277 to the phone, tries to start the phone's head unit server, and starts the DHU.

If the head unit server is not running, OCTAVE waits up to 60 seconds for you to start it on the phone, and the screen does not respond during that wait. When the DHU cannot be started, the page shows "Could not start Android Auto" with setup steps and a Try Again button. OCTAVE writes the DHU's settings file, ~/.android/headunit.ini, each time it starts it (1920x1080, 160 dpi).

iPhone (AirPlay)

An iPhone cannot be mirrored the way an Android phone is: iOS has nothing like adb, no program can capture its screen or fake touches from outside, and CarPlay needs an authentication chip from Apple. What every iPhone does have is AirPlay, the feature that sends its screen to an Apple TV. OCTAVE is an AirPlay receiver, so the iPhone mirrors its screen to OCTAVE, or plays its music through it, with nothing installed on the phone.

AirPlay sends no touches back. So OCTAVE also pretends to be a Bluetooth mouse: with the iPhone's AssistiveTouch pointer on, a tap on the mirrored picture moves that pointer there and clicks.

The iPhone card only appears in Settings > Phone in builds that have AirPlay: Linux, the Raspberry Pi and Orange Pi included. Touch needs Bluetooth on the OCTAVE machine.

Setting It Up

  1. Settings > Phone > iPhone: turn on AirPlay Receiver. It is off by default because it announces OCTAVE on the local network.
  2. Put the iPhone on the same Wi-Fi network as OCTAVE. In a car that means the head unit joins the phone's hotspot, or the phone joins the head unit's.
  3. On the iPhone: Control Center > Screen Mirroring > OCTAVE. For music only, pick OCTAVE as the speaker in Control Center's music card instead.
  4. The first time, the iPhone asks for a PIN. OCTAVE opens the iPhone page with the PIN in large digits; it is also in the first line of the iPhone settings card ("Type 1234 on the iPhone."). The iPhone is remembered after that.
  5. When mirroring starts, OCTAVE opens the iPhone page by itself. The X leaves the page; the iPhone keeps mirroring until you stop it on the phone.

Touch Through a Bluetooth Mouse

With Touch Through Bluetooth on (the default), OCTAVE offers itself as a Bluetooth mouse named "OCTAVE Touch" (your receiver name plus "Touch"). Pair it once:

  1. Tap Pair Touch in the settings card, or on the iPhone page. For three minutes OCTAVE accepts a pairing. At any other time it refuses, so a stray Bluetooth device cannot pair with the head unit.
  2. On the iPhone: Settings > Bluetooth, tap OCTAVE Touch.
  3. On the iPhone: Settings > Accessibility > Touch > AssistiveTouch, turn it on. A grey pointer appears.

After that it reconnects by itself. A tap on the mirrored picture clicks there, a drag drags, and a mouse wheel on OCTAVE scrolls. Only one finger is followed. If taps land in the wrong place, start mirroring and tap Calibrate touch on the iPhone page: OCTAVE moves the pointer to a known spot and asks you to tap where it shows. How far the pointer moves depends on the iPhone and its tracking speed, and this measures it.

Limits

  • Linux only.
  • No AirPlay video (sending a film to the screen without mirroring) and no H.265.
  • Music over AirPlay is not one of the music sources yet: OCTAVE's own music keeps playing under it, and OCTAVE's music controls do not drive the iPhone.
  • Everything that needs a real iPhone is still to be checked on one. The list is in TODO/iphone-airplay.md.

How It Works

From here on the page is for people who want to know what happens underneath, or who are changing the code.

The three features differ in how they reach the phone and are the same after that. Each receives H.264 video (the usual compressed video format), decodes it with one shared decoder (H264Decoder, built on FFmpeg's libavcodec) and hands the frames to the video element on the page. Each plays the phone's sound through OCTAVE's audio output, at the volume and mute of the volume system. Each sends touches back in its own way.

Phone Mirror: the Pieces

The problem: show an Android phone's screen with low delay and real multitouch, with nothing installed on the phone and no extra programs on the OCTAVE machine. The open-source tool scrcpy solves the phone's half of this with a small Java program, its server, that adb starts on the phone. It captures the screen, compresses it with the phone's hardware encoder, and injects touches. OCTAVE uses its own fork of that server and has its own client built in, so there is no scrcpy to install.

PieceJob
PhoneMirrorManagerWhat the rest of the app talks to. Finds adb and the phone, decides when to start and restart, holds the settings, the Wi-Fi pairing and the phone's music state. Every adb command runs on worker threads, so a dead link never freezes the screen.
ScrcpyClientOne session: sends the server to the phone and starts it, opens the connections, decodes the video, plays the sound, sends touches and keys.
The phone server (phone_server/)The Java program running on the phone, as the adb shell user. It cannot be an installed app: only a program started by adb has the rights it needs.
PhoneMirrorWidgetThe phone window in QML: the picture, the touch area, the Connect Phone button.

Phone Mirror: How a Session Starts

  1. Find adb and the phone. adb is looked for next to the OCTAVE program first (releases bundle it), then on the PATH, then in the usual Android SDK folders. A version new enough to pair over Wi-Fi (platform-tools 30) is preferred. Then adb devices lists the phones. With several, the one chosen with selectDevice() wins, then a USB phone, then a Wi-Fi one.
  2. Send the server. The server is built into the OCTAVE program. It is copied to the phone's temporary folder at every start, because the server is told to delete its file when it exits.
  3. Open a tunnel. adb forward connects a free local port to the server's socket on the phone. Tunnels left by an OCTAVE that was killed are removed first.
  4. Start the server through adb shell, with the display to capture: a new virtual display at the size of the phone window (Desktop mode), or the phone's own screen.
  5. Connect three times through the tunnel: one connection for video, one for sound, one for control. The server waits for all three before sending anything. OCTAVE allows 20 seconds from the tap.
  6. Stream. The server names the phone and the picture size, and frames follow. The session state becomes streaming.
  7. End. A stop you asked for tells the server to end and removes the tunnel. A link that drops is handled differently: see Dropped Links.

Home does not run any adb command on an install that has never mirrored a phone, until you tap Connect Phone. Any adb command starts adb's background server, and on Windows that raises a firewall prompt. The exceptions cost nothing: on Linux OCTAVE can see a phone with USB debugging on the USB bus without adb, and on any system it checks once after launch whether an adb server is already running.

In Desktop mode the virtual display is the phone window's size in pixels, scaled down to at most 1920 on the long side. When the window changes shape, the session restarts at the new size once the size has been steady for a second and a half, and only for a real change (more than 3 % in shape or 15 % in size). A smaller change is scaled to fit, because a restart costs seconds without a picture. The Media Room's phone never resizes a running session: it shows what Home started, so moving between the two pages does not restart anything.

The Picture

  • One view has the picture at a time. The manager has a single videoSink, and the phone window on screen takes it. The client keeps decoding with no view attached and gives a new view the latest frame at once. This matters because the phone only sends a frame when its screen changes, and asking it for a fresh one restarts its encoder.
  • The last frame stays up. OCTAVE never clears the picture. While the phone is waking, reconnecting or restarting, the last good frame is shown.
  • Black frames are held back. A dozing phone sends black frames, and the first frame after a reconnect is black with a strip of status icons. The client checks how bright each frame is and does not show a black one for up to 3 seconds. A screen that stays black longer than that really is black, and is shown.
  • The decoder adds no delay of its own. It is set up to return each frame as soon as it arrives, and frames go to the screen in the phone's own colour format, converted on the graphics card.

Sound and Ducking

On Android 11 and newer the server captures everything the phone plays, which also silences the phone's own speaker, and sends it uncompressed. In OCTAVE the sound goes through three steps:

  1. Gain. The Phone Audio Level setting is applied, through a soft limiter so a loud source does not clip harshly.
  2. A cushion. Sound shares one adb link with the picture, so a late packet would be heard as a gap. OCTAVE keeps some sound queued ahead of the speaker: 200 ms over USB, 400 ms over Wi-Fi. Each time the output runs dry the cushion doubles, up to 800 ms, and what a link needed is remembered for the next session of that run. The cost is that sound trails the picture by that much.
  3. Volume. The result plays on the audio output chosen in OCTAVE's settings, at OCTAVE's volume and mute.

Ducking means turning one sound down while another plays. The client watches whether the phone is producing sound right now (phoneMirrorManager.audioPlaying; a second and a half of silence ends it, which is long enough to bridge the gaps between navigation sentences). While it is, and Duck Local Music is on, MediaManager lowers OCTAVE's own music to the duck level, and brings it back afterwards. The same sound feeds the visualizer while the phone is the music source.

Picture Quality

The bit rate is how much data per second the phone spends on the picture: more is sharper, and too much makes frames arrive late. OCTAVE sets it by itself. For every packet it compares when the phone made it with when it arrived. If packets start arriving later than they do on a quiet link, they are queueing somewhere: the link is too slow, or OCTAVE's decoder cannot keep up.

LinkStarts atCeilingFloor
USB16 Mbps32 Mbps2 Mbps
Wi-Fi8 Mbps20 Mbps2 Mbps

When packets queue for more than 150 ms on average over a second, the rate is cut to 70 %. When two seconds in a row are clear, at least 5 seconds after the last cut, and the picture is actually using the rate it has, it is raised by 10 %. The phone's encoder is told the new rate while it runs, with no restart. The rate in use is phoneMirrorManager.currentBitRate, and every change is logged. A slow link therefore costs sharpness, not smoothness.

Touch and the Home Key

The phone window forwards every finger (up to ten; a mouse counts as one) as press, move and release, in positions from 0 to 1 across the picture: injectTouch(pointerId, action, x, y). A touch that starts on the black bars around the picture is ignored for its whole life, so a thumb resting on the frame cannot tap the phone's edge. The phone window also tells the rest of the app that a sideways swipe on it is the phone's, so it never changes page.

pressBack(), pressHome() and pressAppSwitch() send Android's navigation keys. On a virtual display Android sends the Home key to the phone's own screen, where it does nothing useful, so pressHome() starts the phone's launcher on the virtual display. In Desktop mode the on-screen keyboard is told to open on the virtual display too, not on the phone's own screen.

Keeping the Phone Awake

A virtual display goes to sleep with the phone: when the phone dozes, the display stops drawing and touches are dropped, with nothing on the connection to say so. The only way to keep mirroring is to wake the phone again. A part of the phone server called the keeper does this from inside the phone, where it reacts within a frame or two. OCTAVE only sends it the policy (the Phone Screen Off While Mirroring setting and the 8-second grace time) and shows what it reports.

  • It watches whether the phone is awake and whether it is locked, four times a second.
  • It resets the phone's idle timer every second and a half, so the phone never dims or times out while mirrored, on the charger or off it.
  • It decides what a power press meant, by the rules in The Phone's Own Screen and Power Button. A phone you unlocked is "in use": its screen is left alone until you lock it.
  • It darkens the screen with the display's power mode, not by sleeping, so the stream and touch keep working. The server restores the screen when it exits.
  • It reports every change. OCTAVE publishes it as phoneAsleep and phoneInUse, and holds the last frame while the phone is asleep.

The keeper belongs to the server process, not to one connection, so what it knows survives a reconnect. resumeMirroring() takes a phone back from "in use", and wakePhone() sends a wake key over adb as a fallback.

Dropped Links

In a car the phone gets picked up, put down, and its cable nudged. Each time, the USB link drops for a moment. Over Wi-Fi the link can fade as well. Four things make that nearly invisible:

  • Both sides notice a dead link within seconds. A cable that comes out closes the connection, and both sides see it at once. A Wi-Fi link that fades out does not: the connection stays open and silent, and the operating system only gives up after many minutes. So OCTAVE sends the phone a ping every 2 seconds and the phone answers. When OCTAVE gets no answer for 8 seconds, it treats the link as dropped and starts reconnecting. When the phone gets no ping for 8 seconds, it ends the session, and stops keeping itself awake for a head unit that is no longer there.
  • The server waits for OCTAVE to come back. In Desktop mode the server does not exit when its client disappears. It keeps the virtual display, with your apps as you left them, for 60 seconds, and a client that reconnects in that time picks it up. Without this you would land on an empty desktop after every blip. OCTAVE only reattaches to the same phone, whichever link it comes back on, so a cable pull can carry on over Wi-Fi.
  • The music keeps playing. The server also keeps its hold on the phone's sound for 10 seconds, throwing it away. The phone's speaker stays silent and the song carries on when OCTAVE is back. A stop you asked for releases it at once and pauses the phone's music.
  • OCTAVE reconnects quietly. After a drop in the middle of a stream it tries again at once, then every second for 10 seconds, whichever page is on screen, since the phone's music may be playing. The phone window keeps the last picture, and the music controls keep the track. After 10 seconds it gives up being quiet and waits for the phone, looking every 2 seconds while a phone window is on screen.

The session state is phoneMirrorManager.sessionState:

StateMeaning
idleNot started, or stopped.
startingA session is being set up.
streamingThe picture is up.
reconnectingThe link dropped mid-stream; the quiet 10 seconds.
waitingForPhoneNo usable phone. Restarts when one appears.
failedA setup problem or a failure retrying cannot fix. Not retried.

lastErrorKind says what the last error was about: noPhone, device (unauthorized, offline, disconnected), setup or fatal. The message itself comes with the scrcpyError signal. If the phone's hardware video encoder fails, OCTAVE restarts once with Android's software encoder and keeps using it for that phone until OCTAVE restarts.

Wi-Fi Underneath

adb carries the whole session over the network just as it does over USB, so nothing in the client changes for Wi-Fi. The manager's only job is to get the phone into adb's list of devices and keep it there.

  • Joining the car's Wi-Fi. Before either cable step below, the manager reads the phone's Wi-Fi address over the cable. With Car Wi-Fi set, it first asks whether this machine hosts that network: on Linux, an access point with that name in iw dev. If it does, the phone must be on that network. If it does not, any network this machine is on will do. When the phone's address does not fit, the manager runs cmd wifi connect-network in the phone's shell with the network's name and password, then waits up to 10 seconds for the phone to get an address there. The command asks for a fixed MAC address (-r persistent), so the network gives the phone the same IP address every time. An Android that does not know that option is asked again without it. Joined this way, the network does not become the phone's route to the internet. A phone plugged in while it is already mirrored over Wi-Fi is moved in the background; the session drops for a moment and comes back on the cable. If the session was still starting over Wi-Fi, it starts again on the cable.
  • The watcher is a shell script, /data/local/tmp/octave-wifi-watch.sh on the phone. Only a machine that hosts the Car Wi-Fi network installs it. At every session start the manager asks the phone whether the watcher of the current version is running (the version is a hash of the script, so a new name or password replaces it). If not, it copies the script over and starts it detached from adb. It has to ask every time: pulling a USB cable restarts the phone's adb service, which ends the watcher, and the session that follows over Wi-Fi starts it again. The script holds the network's password; the file is readable only by the phone's shell user. It joins only when the network's scan result is under 120 seconds old and stronger than -70 dBm.
  • After a cable session. A computer the phone approved over USB is accepted by its Wireless debugging too, with no pairing. So OCTAVE reads the phone's Wi-Fi address over the cable, switches Wireless debugging on, finds its port, and connects. The session does not wait for this.
  • QR pairing. OCTAVE shows a QR code holding a random service name and password, in the format Android Studio uses. The phone scans it and announces a pairing service under that name on the network. OCTAVE finds it, runs adb pair and then adb connect, and reports the outcome with the wirelessResult signal. Needs platform-tools 30 or newer; with an older adb pairing fails at once and says so.
  • Stay Wireless After USB runs adb tcpip 5555 before a USB session. This restarts the phone's adb service, so the session starts a couple of seconds later. The phone then listens on port 5555 on every network until it restarts, which is why the setting carries a warning.
  • Reconnecting. At startup, and whenever no phone is found, the manager tries the remembered addresses, at most every 5 seconds. It keeps one address for each network the phone was reached on (up to six, the scrcpyWirelessKnown setting) and tries those on a network this machine is on now, most recent first. Wireless debugging takes a new port each time it is switched on, so when a saved address fails OCTAVE looks the phone up on the network (mDNS) and remembers the new port. adb can also hold a connection to a phone that has not allowed this computer, and it keeps that connection as refused even after the phone has allowed it. So when adb reports a Wi-Fi connection as refused or offline, OCTAVE drops it and connects again: at once at every cable session, which is where the phone usually gives its approval, and every 30 seconds while reconnecting. A phone that allows this computer is then usable straight away, and one that does not is made to ask again.
  • Finding phones on the network uses adb's own mDNS. Where the installed adb was built without it, OCTAVE falls back to avahi-browse on Linux, if it is installed.

Rule: the version string must match on both sides
The server refuses a client whose version string is not exactly its own (3.3.4-octave). SCRCPY_VERSION_NAME in phone_server/build_without_gradle.sh and kServerVersion in src/phone_mirror/scrcpyclient.h change together. After editing anything under phone_server/src, rebuild the jar with that script and commit it (tools/phone-server/octave-phone-server) with the source: the program embeds the committed jar, not the source. The phone-server CI job rebuilds it and fails if either jar lacks the server class or the version string. See Building.

Bundled adb. scripts/fetch_platform_tools.py downloads Google's platform-tools into tools/platform-tools/, and the Windows, macOS and Linux x86-64 releases ship it next to the program. Google publishes no ARM Linux build, so on a Raspberry Pi or Orange Pi install adb from the system's packages (sudo apt install adb).

Built-in Android Auto

Android Auto's protocol (AAP) is a set of numbered channels over one encrypted connection: one for video, one for each kind of sound, one for touch, and so on. OCTAVE plays the head unit's part.

  1. Find the phone over adb, off the main thread, choosing it the way Phone Mirror does.
  2. Check the head unit server. OCTAVE looks at the phone's list of listening network ports for port 5277. Asking Android's service list is not reliable: it cannot tell a running server from a stale record.
  3. Forward a port. adb forward picks a free local port and connects it to the phone's 5277, so it never collides with the DHU's or Phone Mirror's. It is removed when the session ends.
  4. Connect. AapConnection, on its own thread, exchanges versions, sets up an encrypted connection (TLS 1.2) with your certificate, and then tells the phone what this head unit has: a screen, three sound outputs, a microphone, a touchscreen with media keys, sensors for night mode and driving status, and a now-playing display.
  5. Stream. Video goes through H264Decoder to the page. Each kind of sound has its own output, with at most half a second queued. OCTAVE acknowledges every frame and pings the phone every second and a half; ten seconds without an answer ends the session.
  6. Music. The phone's requests to play go to nowPlaying, which answers them by the rules in Which Source Plays the Music. OCTAVE's play / pause / next / previous go out as media key presses.
  7. End. Closing the page or quitting OCTAVE says goodbye and waits for the phone's answer (up to 3 seconds at quit). A session cut off without it can leave the phone's head unit server accepting connections and never answering.

While the page is off screen OCTAVE tells the phone, and the phone stops sending video; sound carries on. The built-in head unit's properties and methods are marked experimental in the API: they may still change. The message formats, as checked against a real phone, are in docs/ANDROID_AUTO_PROTOCOL.md, and what is left to do is in TODO/android-auto-native-receiver.md.

AirPlay

The AirPlay protocol itself is UxPlay's library, an open-source AirPlay receiver, compiled into OCTAVE from third_party/uxplay/. It handles pairing, the encrypted streams and announcing OCTAVE on the network, with its own announcer, so no Avahi or Bonjour service is needed. OCTAVE's AirPlayReceiver does what UxPlay's own video and audio players would:

  • Video goes through the shared H264Decoder to the page.
  • Sound is decoded with libavcodec and played at OCTAVE's volume multiplied by the iPhone's own volume slider. Muting OCTAVE mutes it.
  • Track details the iPhone sends with music appear as airPlayManager.title, artist and album.

The pointer. iOS accepts only relative mouse movement ("move 5 right"), never "go to this spot", so OCTAVE has to know where the pointer is. For the first tap it pushes the pointer hard into the top-left corner, after which its position is certain, then moves to the target in small steps (small steps avoid iOS's pointer acceleration) and clicks. Later taps start from where the last one ended. The position is forgotten when the picture changes size, as in a rotation, or the mouse reconnects. The mouse is a Bluetooth LE device registered with BlueZ, Linux's Bluetooth service, without root.

The receiver's identity and the iPhones that have paired are kept in airplay/state.json in the config folder, and its key in airplay/receiver.pem.

Why It Is Built This Way

  • A built-in client, not the scrcpy program. Depending on an installed scrcpy means depending on whatever version a system ships, plus a way to get its window into OCTAVE. The server on the phone is the part that is hard to write and does not need replacing. The client is small, and owning it gives a picture in under a second, touch with no added delay, and nothing for the user to install.
  • A fork of the server, not a pinned copy. The problems of a car (a power press that puts the display to sleep, a USB blip that destroys the desktop, music that must survive a cable pull) need behaviour on the phone that scrcpy's server does not have.
  • A virtual display by default. It gives a picture in the shape of the dash, not of a phone held upright, and leaves the phone's own screen free and dark. The costs are known and handled: the Home key needs a workaround, the display sleeps with the phone, and it needs Android 11.
  • The keeper lives on the phone. Done from OCTAVE, noticing a sleeping phone and waking it took about a second of black picture per press. Inside the phone it takes a frame or two.
  • The phone's volume is never changed. Gain is applied to the sound inside OCTAVE. A crash in the car must not leave the phone at full volume.
  • Hold the last frame; never clear the picture. Every change of state has a moment with no good frame. The last good frame is the right thing to show until a real one arrives, and messages appear only when the wait is long enough to need explaining.
  • Uncompressed sound. It costs little next to the video and means there is no audio decoder to go wrong.
  • The bit rate is automatic. A fixed setting is wrong for somebody's link. Measuring whether frames arrive on time is right for all of them.
  • QR pairing is the only wireless setup on screen. Typing codes and addresses on a car screen is worse than pointing a camera at it, and a cable session needs no setup at all.
  • No certificate is shipped for Android Auto. OCTAVE has no right to distribute one, so the user supplies it.
  • Android Auto has to be asked for. A phone that starts music whenever it connects would override what the driver chose every time the car starts.
  • Bluetooth pairing only inside a window the user opens. A head unit that accepts any pairing request is a head unit anyone nearby can attach a keyboard to.
